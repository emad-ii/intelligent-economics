Euler equation forced

Configurations read as contingent plans. An interior mode of $\rho^*$ satisfies $\nabla V=-\tau\nabla\log\mu$. The $\tau\to 0$ mode is the first-order condition of dynamic optimisation, the Euler equation its intertemporal component. At finite $\tau$ the conditions carry the definite correction $-\tau\nabla\log\mu$.

The paper recovers the Euler equation as the zero-temperature mode of the forced object (Table 3; section 5). Transversality is supplied as a boundary condition. What $\tau>0$ returns is the doxic score at the price of information.

Drag $\tau$ down. The mode of $\rho^*$ walks onto the value peak and the correction vanishes: that is the forced Euler. Raise $\tau$, or move the doxic peak, and $-\tau\nabla\log\mu$ shifts the stationarity condition by a definite signed amount — the pull of the reference at the price of information.

What the paper states

From section 5: with configurations read as contingent plans (the premise lists plans among them) an interior mode of $\rho^*$ satisfies $\nabla V=-\tau\nabla\log\mu$, so the $\tau\to 0$ mode satisfies the first-order conditions of dynamic optimisation, the Euler equation their intertemporal component, with transversality supplied as a boundary condition. At finite $\tau$ the conditions carry the definite correction $-\tau\nabla\log\mu$, the doxic score at the price of information.

Table 3 lists the recovery as forced, recovered by the $\tau\to 0$ mode over contingent plans, and the interest it returns as the correction $-\tau\nabla\log\mu$. The site recovery map on ie.ii.inc names the same row. The paper does not write a consumption-Euler specialisation; that setting-specific construction is not added here.
